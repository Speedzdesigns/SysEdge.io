How We Recovered From Ransomware in 48 Hours — Without Paying the Ransom
Jason Taylor  •  June 2025  •  12 min read

It was 2:17 PM on a Tuesday when the help desk tickets started flooding in. "I can't open any files." "My desktop looks different." "There's a message on my screen asking for Bitcoin."

We had been hit by ransomware. And by the time the first ticket came in, it had already been running for an estimated 90 minutes.

This is the story of how we recovered completely — no ransom paid, full operations restored in 48 hours — and the lessons that every IT pro needs to hear before it happens to them.

The First 30 Minutes: Containment

The single most important thing you do in a ransomware event is stop the bleeding. Ransomware spreads laterally. Every minute it runs, more files are encrypted.

Our immediate steps:

  • Pulled network cables on any machine showing infection symptoms
  • Disabled the affected AD accounts to stop credential reuse
  • Took snapshots of any unaffected VMs before the spread could reach them
  • Called our cyber insurance carrier — this is step one that most people skip
💡 If you remember nothing else from this article: pull the network cable first, ask questions second. A disconnected machine can't spread ransomware. An online machine can.

What Saved Us: Immutable Backups

The reason we recovered without paying was simple: our Veeam backups were stored in an immutable S3-compatible repository. The ransomware couldn't touch them because they were write-once by design.

Many organizations think they have backups. What they actually have is a shared network drive that the ransomware encrypted along with everything else.

Try Veeam Free →

The Recovery Timeline

Hour 1–3: Containment, insurance call, forensic snapshot of encrypted machines. Identified Patient Zero (a phishing email opened by an accounting employee).

Hour 3–8: Began VM restoration from immutable backups. Priority order: domain controllers first, then file servers, then workstations.

Hour 8–16: Restored 80% of critical systems. Rebuilt affected workstations from golden image rather than trying to clean them.

Hour 16–36: Restored remaining systems, validated data integrity, re-enabled user accounts with forced password resets and MFA enrollment.

Hour 36–48: Full operations restored. Post-incident documentation and reporting to cyber insurance.

The Three Things That Would Have Made It Worse

  • Backups on the same network with no air gap. We almost made this mistake six months earlier. An audit caught it.
  • No documented recovery runbook. At 3am you cannot afford to be figuring out your recovery process. It needs to be written down.
  • SMS-based MFA. The attacker got in via compromised credentials. Hardware MFA or an authenticator app would have stopped it cold.

What to Put in Place Before It Happens to You

  1. Immutable backups — Veeam with object lock, or a dedicated backup appliance with air-gap capability
  2. Hardware MFA or TOTP authenticator apps — no SMS
  3. Documented incident response runbook — who to call, in what order, what to do
  4. Cyber liability insurance — it pays for forensics, notification, and legal fees
  5. EDR on every endpoint — not just AV
The $40/month we spent on immutable cloud backup saved us from a $150,000+ ransom demand. The ROI math on backup is not complicated.

CMMC 2.0 for Small IT Teams: What You Actually Need to Do
Jason Taylor  •  May 2025  •  9 min read

If your organization works with the Department of Defense — or supports a contractor that does — CMMC 2.0 is no longer optional. And yet most of the guidance out there was written for compliance consultants charging $300/hour, not the IT admin who has to actually implement it.

This guide is for the practitioners. Here's what CMMC 2.0 actually requires, in plain English.

The Three Levels (And Which One Applies to You)

Level 1 (Foundational): 17 basic practices. Applies if you handle Federal Contract Information (FCI). This is the baseline — things like using antivirus, limiting physical access, and maintaining clean user accounts. Most already-decent IT shops are 80% there.

Level 2 (Advanced): 110 practices aligned with NIST SP 800-171. Required if you handle Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI). This is where most organizations need real work.

Level 3 (Expert): Based on NIST SP 800-172. Only for the most sensitive DoD programs. If you're Level 3, you already know it.

The 5 Level 1 Controls Most Teams Are Missing

  • Documented access control policy (not just practice — written down)
  • Sanitization procedure for media disposal (that old hard drive in the closet is a liability)
  • Malicious code protection on all endpoints and email gateways
  • A process for scanning external files and media before use
  • Physical access controls with visitor logs

Where Level 2 Gets Hard

The jump from Level 1 to Level 2 is significant. The 110 controls in NIST 800-171 require things most SMBs haven't formalized: system security plans (SSPs), audit log management, incident response procedures, and configuration management.

💡 Start with a System Security Plan (SSP). It's the single document that maps every NIST 800-171 control to how your organization meets it. Without an SSP, you can't prove compliance even if you're doing everything right.

Tools That Help With CMMC 2.0

You'll need solid answers for: identity management, audit logging, endpoint protection, vulnerability scanning, and media protection. Here's what I recommend at the SMB/small contractor scale:

  • Identity: Azure AD (now Entra ID) with MFA enforced — maps cleanly to multiple controls
  • Endpoint: Microsoft Defender for Business or CrowdStrike Falcon Go
  • Audit logging: A SIEM — even a basic one. Datto EDR has a solid SMB option.
  • Vulnerability scanning: Tenable.io or Qualys (both have SMB tiers)

CMMC is achievable for small teams. It requires documentation discipline more than expensive tooling. Start with your SSP, map your current controls honestly, and work the gap list systematically.

Build a Home Lab for Under $300: The Setup Every IT Pro Should Have
Jason Taylor  •  March 2025  •  14 min read

The fastest way to level up your IT career isn't certifications. It's building things, breaking them, and fixing them — in an environment where you're not terrified of impacting production.

That's what a home lab gives you. And you don't need a rack of enterprise gear to do it right.

The $300 Shopping List

  • Mini PC (Beelink SER5 or similar) — ~$180: AMD Ryzen 5, 16GB RAM, 500GB NVMe. More than enough to run 4–6 VMs simultaneously with Proxmox.
  • Managed switch — ~$45: TP-Link TL-SG108E. 8-port, VLAN capable, and dead simple to configure.
  • Cables, USB drive for install — ~$20: Nothing exotic needed.
  • Cloud storage for backups — ~$5/mo: Backblaze B2. Dirt cheap and S3-compatible.

The Software Stack (All Free)

  • Proxmox VE — type-1 hypervisor, excellent UI, handles both VMs and containers
  • pfSense — enterprise-grade firewall as a VM. Learn the same concepts used in real deployments.
  • Windows Server Evaluation — free 180-day evaluation licenses from Microsoft. Build your AD domain.
  • Veeam Community Edition — free backup for up to 10 VMs. Use it to practice your backup/restore workflow.
  • Kali Linux — practice security scanning on your own network, legally.
💡 The Proxmox + pfSense combination alone is worth the cost of the whole setup. You'll understand networking concepts at a level that no course can teach you.

What to Build First

  1. Install Proxmox on the mini PC
  2. Deploy pfSense as a VM — configure WAN/LAN, set up VLANs on your managed switch
  3. Stand up a Windows Server VM — install AD DS, promote to domain controller
  4. Join a Windows 10/11 VM to the domain — create GPOs, practice AD administration
  5. Deploy Veeam, set up backup jobs to Backblaze B2 — then practice restoring

What You'll Learn That Certifications Won't Teach You

Running a real (if small) network teaches you things no exam can: why DNS is always the answer, how AD replication actually works, what a misconfigured VLAN looks like in practice, and the real difference between a backup and a tested, restorable backup.

Build the lab. Break things. Fix them. Repeat. Your next job interview will be noticeably different.
